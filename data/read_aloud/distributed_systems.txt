This is a tour of replicated storage, from the consensus layer at the bottom to the consistency guarantees users actually see at the top.

Begin with agreement. A group of machines must agree on a single ordered log of operations, even though machines crash and networks drop packets. Paxos was the first protocol to solve this correctly, and it is notoriously difficult to implement from the paper. Raft was designed later with understandability as an explicit goal. Raft decomposes the problem into leader election, log replication, and safety, and that decomposition is the reason almost every new system reaches for Raft rather than Paxos. Both protocols require a quorum, meaning a strict majority of the configured members, which is why cluster sizes are almost always odd numbers.

Leader election is where the subtle bugs live. A leader that pauses for a long garbage collection cycle may wake up believing it is still the leader while the cluster has already elected a successor. That is split brain, and the standard defense is a fencing token, a monotonically increasing number that storage nodes use to reject writes from a stale leader. Without a fencing token, a slow leader can corrupt data long after it has logically been replaced.

Byzantine fault tolerance is a stronger and more expensive model. Raft and Paxos assume that machines either work correctly or stop. Byzantine protocols assume machines may lie, which raises the quorum requirement and the message complexity substantially. Almost no internal infrastructure needs Byzantine fault tolerance, and paying for it without needing it is a common architectural mistake.

Ordering without a leader requires different machinery. A Lamport clock is a simple counter that captures the happens before relation, and a vector clock extends that to detect genuine concurrency between events. Systems in the Dynamo lineage, including Cassandra and Riak, use vector clocks and accept that concurrent writes produce siblings that the application must reconcile. That design trades a hard problem in the database for a hard problem in the application, and teams underestimate the cost of that trade with remarkable consistency.

Anti-entropy is how replicas that have drifted apart converge again. A Merkle tree lets two replicas compare enormous key ranges by exchanging a small number of hashes, descending only into subtrees whose hashes disagree. Hinted handoff covers the shorter outage, where a coordinator temporarily stores writes destined for an unreachable replica and delivers them when it recovers. Read repair fixes divergence opportunistically during a normal read. Gossip protocols spread membership and failure information without any central coordinator.

Now the storage engine. A write ahead log makes durability cheap by turning random writes into sequential appends. A log structured merge tree buffers writes in memory, flushes sorted runs to disk, and compacts them in the background. Compaction is the dominant cost, and a poorly tuned compaction strategy will produce write amplification that dwarfs the actual workload. A bloom filter sits in front of each sorted run so that a lookup for a missing key usually avoids disk entirely. Deletes are not deletes. A delete writes a tombstone, and the tombstone must survive until every replica has seen it, which is why a cluster that deletes heavily can grow rather than shrink.

Partitioning determines whether the system scales. Consistent hashing distributes keys across nodes so that adding a node moves only a small fraction of the data. It does not protect against a hot shard, where one key or one narrow range absorbs a disproportionate share of traffic. Hot shards are usually a data modeling problem wearing an infrastructure costume.

Finally, the guarantees. Linearizability means every operation appears to take effect at a single instant between its invocation and its response, and it is the strongest and most expensive single object guarantee. Serializability concerns transactions rather than single objects, and requires only that some serial order exists. Snapshot isolation lets readers see a consistent view without blocking writers, and permits write skew, which surprises people who assume it is equivalent to serializability. Below those sit the session guarantees, including monotonic reads and read your writes, which are weak on paper but sufficient for a surprising number of real applications.

Multi version concurrency control is the mechanism underneath most of this. Readers see an older version rather than waiting for a lock, so read heavy workloads scale well and the cost moves to version cleanup.

The recurring lesson is that each layer buys a guarantee with a cost, and systems fail most often when a team adopts the guarantee without budgeting for the cost.
